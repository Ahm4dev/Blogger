I.1

Purpose and definition of internal auditing
"Internal auditing strengthens the organization's ability to create, protect, and sustain value by providing the board and management with independent, risk-based, and objective assurance, advice, insight, and foresight."Purpose of Internal Auditing, Domain I of the Global Internal Audit Standards

Internal auditing enhances the organization's:

Successful achievement of its objectives.

Governance, risk management, and control processes.

Decision-making and oversight.

Reputation and credibility with its stakeholders.

Ability to serve the public interest.

Internal auditing is most effective when:

  • It is performed by competent professionals in conformance with the Global Internal Audit Standards, which are set in the public interest.
  • The internal audit function is independently positioned with direct accountability to the board.
  • Internal auditors are free from undue influence and committed to making objective assessments.
Definition (Glossary)Internal audit services are assurance and advisory services. The internal audit function is a professional individual or group responsible for providing them; it may be in-house, outsourced, or co-sourced (a mix).
Key exam pointThe purpose statement names two audiences: the board and management. It lists three qualities (independent, risk-based, objective) and four outputs (assurance, advice, insight, foresight). "Foresight" means helping the organization anticipate emerging risks and opportunities, which is new emphasis compared with the 2017 Mission.

I.2

The International Professional Practices Framework (IPPF)

The IPPF is The IIA's framework that organizes its authoritative guidance. Since January 2024 it has three components. The first two are mandatory; the third is recommended.

Anatomy of each Standard

1 · Requirements

Mandatory practices. Written with "must". Conformance is required.

2 · Considerations for Implementation

Common and preferred practices to consider. Not mandatory but explain how to meet the requirements.

3 · Examples of Evidence of Conformance

Ways to demonstrate that the requirements were met (documents, minutes, plans, surveys).

WordMeaning in the Standards
MUSTAn unconditional requirement.
SHOULDConformance is expected unless, using professional judgment, circumstances justify deviation.
MAYOptional practices.

Who must conform

  • The Standards apply to any individual or function that provides internal audit services, whether in the public, private or non-profit sector, in-house, outsourced or co-sourced, and whatever the size of the function.
  • Responsibility for conformance rests with the chief audit executive (CAE) for the function and with each internal auditor for their own work. Domain III also lists essential conditions for the board and senior management.
  • When internal auditors also follow other professional bodies' requirements (e.g., government auditing standards, ISAs), they may cite those too.

Topical Requirements

  • Mandatory requirements that set a minimum baseline for assessing a specific risk area (e.g., Cybersecurity; others in development include Third-Party Risk and Organizational Behavior).
  • They apply when the subject is in the audit plan, when it is identified during an engagement, or when an engagement is requested on the topic.
  • If the internal auditor decides a requirement does not apply, that decision and its rationale must be documented. Conformance is evaluated in quality assessments.
Exam trap"Global Guidance" (Practice Guides, GTAGs) is not mandatory. If a question asks what is required, choose the Standards or Topical Requirements, never a Practice Guide. Also note that the old "Implementation Guidance" and "Supplemental Guidance" categories no longer exist; they were merged into the Standards' considerations and into Global Guidance.
Public sectorThe IIA also issued Applying the Global Internal Audit Standards in the Public Sector. It explains how the Standards apply where governance, funding and reporting are set by law (e.g., the "board" may be a legislature or audit committee created by statute).

I.4

Mandate, charter and support (Principle 6: Authorized by the Board)
Definitions
  • Mandate: the internal audit function's authority, role, and responsibilities. It may be granted by the board and/or by laws and regulations.
  • Charter: a formal document that includes the function's mandate, organizational position, reporting relationships, scope of work, types of services and other specifications.
  • Board: the highest-level body charged with governance (e.g., board of directors, audit committee, supervisory board, council). If no board exists, "board" refers to a group or person charged with governance, which may include the head of the organization.
6.1 Internal Audit Mandate
  • CAE MUST provide the board and senior management with information to establish the mandate (or confirm it when set by law).
  • CAE coordinates with the board and senior management to understand stakeholders' expectations of assurance and advisory services.
  • The mandate is documented in the charter. CAE periodically reviews whether it still fits strategy and risks.
6.2 Internal Audit Charter
  • CAE MUST develop and maintain a charter; the board approves it.
  • CAE reviews it with the board and senior management when circumstances change (e.g., new strategy, new CAE, regulation) and periodically.
6.3 Board & Senior Management Support
  • CAE MUST help the board and senior management recognize the IA function as a source of independent assurance and advice.
  • Board champions the function; senior management supports access and cooperation.

Minimum content of the internal audit charter

Purpose of Internal Auditing

Commitment to adhering to the Global Internal Audit Standards

Mandate: scope and types of services, board's responsibilities in oversight, and the essential conditions

Organizational position and reporting relationships (functional to the board)

Authority: unrestricted access to records, physical property and personnel

Requirement to communicate impairments and limitations on scope or resources

Board essential conditions under Principle 6 (examples)

  • Discuss with the CAE and senior management the appropriate authority, role and responsibilities of the function.
  • Approve the internal audit charter, and review it periodically.
  • Communicate with the CAE about the board's perspective on strategy, objectives and risks.
  • Ensure the CAE has unrestricted access to the board, and that the function has unrestricted access to data, records, information, personnel and physical properties.
  • Support the function and the CAE (senior management helps make sure there is no interference).
Exam trapWho drafts the charter? The CAE. Who approves it? The board. Senior management is consulted but does not give final approval. If the mandate is set by law, the charter still documents it.

I.5

Organizational independence and the CAE (Principle 7: Positioned Independently)
DefinitionIndependence is freedom from conditions that impair the ability of the internal audit function to carry out internal audit responsibilities in an unbiased manner. It is an attribute of the function (organizational). Objectivity is an unbiased mental attitude of the individual (see Domain II).

7.1 Organizational Independence: requirements

  • The CAE MUST confirm to the board the organizational independence of the IA function at least annually.
  • The CAE reports directly to the board (functional reporting) and to a level within senior management that allows the function to fulfil its mandate (administrative reporting).
  • The CAE must document the reporting relationships and position in the charter, and disclose to the board any interference in determining scope, performing work, or communicating results, and its implications.
  • If the CAE has (or is expected to have) roles and responsibilities beyond internal auditing (e.g., compliance, risk management, ethics), the CAE must discuss them with the board, and safeguards must be put in place. Assurance over those areas must be overseen by a party outside the internal audit function.

Board essential conditions under Principle 7

  • Establish a direct reporting relationship with the CAE and the function.
  • Approve the appointment, removal, remuneration and performance evaluation of the CAE (or participate in these decisions).
  • Meet with the CAE periodically, including in private sessions without senior management present.
  • Protect the function from interference, and engage with senior management on any roles beyond internal auditing.

7.2 CAE Qualifications

  • The board, with senior management, determines the qualifications and competencies needed for the CAE role (e.g., experience, professional certifications such as the CIA, leadership skills).
  • The CAE MUST maintain and enhance the qualifications and competencies needed to fulfil the role.
  • When the CAE is an external service provider (outsourced function), the organization still retains responsibility for an effective IA function, and a person within the organization oversees it.
Key exam pointOrganizational independence is effectively achieved when the CAE reports functionally to the board. Reporting to the CFO or controller as the primary line is a red flag, because those functions are themselves audited. Administrative reporting to the CEO is the preferred pattern.
Exception to rememberA CAE may lead compliance or risk management (common in smaller organizations) only with board awareness and safeguards. The IA function then cannot give assurance on those areas itself; someone outside the function must oversee that assurance.

I.6

Board oversight, resources and quality (Principle 8: Overseen by the Board)
8.1 Board Interaction

The CAE MUST provide the board with the information it needs for oversight: the plan, budget, significant changes, results, conformance, and any risk exposure or control issue requiring board attention. The CAE must also report when essential conditions are not met.

8.2 Resources

The CAE MUST evaluate whether resources (financial, human, technological) are sufficient to fulfil the mandate and achieve the plan, and must inform the board of the impact of insufficient resources.

8.3 Quality

The CAE MUST develop, implement and maintain a quality assurance and improvement program (QAIP) covering all aspects of the function, and communicate its results to the board and senior management at least annually.

8.4 External Quality Assessment

An EQA MUST be performed at least once every five years by a qualified, independent assessor or assessment team. The CAE discusses its scope, frequency and the assessor's qualifications with the board.

External quality assessment details (8.4)

  • Assessor qualifications: at least one member of the team holds an active CIA designation; the team is competent in internal audit practice and the EQA process.
  • Independence of assessor: no actual or perceived conflict of interest; not part of, or under the control of, the organization. (A peer review between two organizations auditing each other in turn is not independent; a review among three or more organizations can be.)
  • The scope must cover conformance with the Standards, the function's performance against its mandate and strategy, and improvement opportunities.
  • Results are communicated to the board and senior management, including the assessor's conclusion on conformance and action plans.

Claiming conformance (Std 4.1 and 8.3)

Allowed

"Conforms with the Global Internal Audit Standards" may be stated only when the results of the QAIP (including the external assessment) support that statement.

Nonconformance

When nonconformance affects the overall scope or operation of the function, the CAE MUST disclose to the board and senior management: the standard(s) not conformed with, the reasons, and the impact on the function and services.

Exam trap: law vs StandardsIf laws or regulations prohibit conformance with part of the Standards, internal auditors must follow the law, conform with all other parts, and disclose the deviation appropriately. The law wins; the Standards do not override legal requirements.

Assurance services vs advisory services
Assurance servicesServices through which internal auditors perform objective assessments to provide confidence about an entity's governance, risk management and control processes over a matter, condition, subject or activity under review relative to established criteria.
Advisory servicesServices through which internal auditors provide advice to an organization's stakeholders without providing assurance or taking on management responsibilities. The nature and scope are agreed with the relevant stakeholders.

Types of advisory services

Advice

Formal or informal input on a process, control design, policy, or system implementation.

Facilitation

Running risk or control self-assessment workshops, benchmarking sessions.

Training

Educating staff on risk, control, fraud awareness, governance.

Other

Preliminary reviews, pre-implementation reviews, investigations support (depending on mandate).

Objectivity and advisory work
  • Internal auditors may provide assurance services where they previously performed advisory services, provided the nature of the advisory work did not impair objectivity and individual objectivity is managed when assigning resources.
  • If an advisory engagement could impair objectivity for later assurance work, the impairment must be disclosed to the client before accepting the engagement.
  • Significant control weaknesses or risks discovered during advisory work should be communicated to senior management and the board when appropriate.
Exam trapAn auditor who designs a control during an advisory engagement and is then asked to audit that control's effectiveness has a self-review threat. The best answer is usually: assign a different auditor, or disclose the impairment. Designing controls and owning or operating them would be a management responsibility, which is never allowed.

II.1

From the Code of Ethics to Domain II, and Principle 1: Integrity

Domain II describes the behavioral expectations for internal auditors. It replaces the former IIA Code of Ethics. It applies to all individuals who provide internal audit services, whether or not they are IIA members or certified. Internal auditors are also expected to follow the ethics of any other profession to which they belong and the organization's own code of conduct.

Principle 1 · Demonstrate IntegrityInternal auditors demonstrate integrity in their work and behavior. Integrity is the foundation of the other principles: it establishes trust and is the basis for reliance on internal auditors' judgment.
1.1 Honesty and Professional Courage
  • Perform work with honesty, and not knowingly make false or misleading statements, or omit information.
  • Exercise professional courage: communicate truthfully and take appropriate action even when confronted by dilemmas, difficult situations, or pressure.
  • The CAE must maintain a work environment where auditors feel supported to express themselves freely and courageously.
1.2 Organization's Ethical Expectations
  • Understand, respect, meet and contribute to the legitimate and ethical expectations of the organization.
  • Be able to recognize conduct that is contrary to those expectations.
  • Encourage and promote an ethics-based culture; report behavior inconsistent with expectations per policy.
1.3 Legal and Ethical Behavior
  • Must not engage in or be party to any activity that is illegal or discreditable to the organization or the profession, or that may harm the organization or its employees.
  • Understand and abide by laws and regulations relevant to the industry and jurisdictions, including making disclosures as required.
  • If the auditor identifies illegal or discreditable behavior, report it in line with policies and laws.
Key exam point"Professional courage" is new vocabulary. In a scenario where senior management pressures the auditor to soften or remove a finding, the integrity answer is to communicate the facts truthfully and, if pressure continues, escalate to the board, not to comply or quietly drop the issue.
Exam trapNot every legal action is ethical. 1.3 requires both legal and ethical behavior. A "legal but discreditable" activity (e.g., publicly criticizing the organization on social media with confidential details) still breaches the principle.

II.2

Principle 2: Maintain Objectivity (threats, impairments, safeguards, disclosure)
DefinitionObjectivity is an unbiased mental attitude that allows internal auditors to make professional judgments, fulfil their responsibilities and achieve the Purpose of Internal Auditing without compromise. Internal auditors do not subordinate their judgment to that of others.
Independence

Organizational attribute of the IA function. Achieved through reporting lines, board oversight, charter. Covered by Principle 7.

vs
Objectivity

Individual attribute of the internal auditor. A state of mind: unbiased, impartial, free of conflicts. Covered by Principle 2.

Threats to objectivity (common categories)

2.1 Individual Objectivity
  • Maintain professional objectivity in all aspects of internal audit services, including decisions on engagement scope, procedures, findings and conclusions.
  • Be aware of and manage potential biases.
  • Avoid conflicts of interest: do not accept anything (gift, payment, favor) that may impair or appear to impair judgment.
2.2 Safeguarding Objectivity
  • Recognize and avoid or mitigate actual, potential and perceived impairments.
  • Refrain from assessing activities for which the auditor was previously responsible; objectivity is presumed impaired if the auditor had that responsibility within the previous 12 months.
  • CAE establishes methodologies to address impairments (e.g., declarations, rotation, supervision).
2.3 Disclosing Impairments
  • If objectivity is impaired in fact or appearance, disclose the details promptly to the appropriate parties (CAE; CAE to board/senior management as needed).
  • If an impairment is discovered after results are communicated, the CAE must discuss it with the relevant parties and communicate the effect on the conclusions, and correct communications if needed.

II.3

Principle 3: Demonstrate Competency (and continuing professional development)
CompetencyThe knowledge, skills and abilities needed to perform internal audit services. Applies to each individual auditor and to the function collectively.
3.1 Competency
  • Internal auditors MUST possess or obtain the competencies to perform their responsibilities.
  • They must engage only in services for which they have (or can obtain) the necessary competencies.
  • If they lack competency, they disclose it to the CAE; the CAE may obtain resources (training, guest auditors, external service providers) or decline the engagement.
  • The CAE ensures the function collectively has the required competencies.
3.2 Continuing Professional Development
  • Internal auditors MUST maintain and continually develop their competencies to improve the effectiveness and quality of services.
  • Pursue CPD consistent with certification requirements (e.g., CIA).
  • Keep up with emerging risks, technologies, regulations, the Standards and Topical Requirements.

II.4

Principle 4: Exercise Due Professional Care (conformance, care, skepticism)
Due professional careApplying the care and skill expected of a reasonably prudent and competent internal auditor in the same or similar circumstances. It does not imply infallibility.
4.1 Conformance with the Standards

Internal auditors MUST plan and perform services in accordance with the Standards. When unable to conform, document and communicate the circumstances, the alternative actions taken, and the impact (to the CAE; the CAE to the board and senior management where relevant).

4.2 Due Professional Care

Internal auditors MUST exercise due professional care by assessing the nature, circumstances and requirements of the services to be provided.

4.3 Professional Skepticism

Internal auditors MUST exercise professional skepticism when planning and performing services.

Considerations when exercising due professional care (4.2)

  1. The organization's strategy and objectives.
  2. The interests of those for whom services are provided and of other stakeholders.
  3. The adequacy and effectiveness of governance, risk management and control processes.
  4. The cost of the services relative to potential benefits.
  5. The extent and timeliness of work needed to achieve the engagement objectives.
  6. The relative complexity, materiality or significance of risks to the activity under review.
  7. The probability of significant errors, fraud, noncompliance and other risks.
  8. The use of appropriate techniques, tools and technology (including data analytics).

Professional skepticism in practice (4.3)

Questioning mind

Maintain an attitude of inquiry; be alert to contradictions.

Critical assessment

Critically assess the reliability of information; corroborate verbal explanations.

Candor

Be honest and candid when raising concerns.

No overreliance

Don't accept management assertions or prior-year conclusions without evidence; don't assume dishonesty either.

Key exam pointDue professional care is reasonable, not absolute. An auditor who performs a well-planned, risk-based engagement and still misses a well-concealed collusive fraud has not necessarily breached due professional care. Questions often test this distinction.
Exam trap: "skepticism" vs "suspicion"Skepticism is neutral: neither assume management is dishonest nor assume unquestioned honesty. Answers that say "assume fraud exists" or "accept management's explanation" are both wrong.

II.5

Principle 5: Maintain Confidentiality

Internal auditors use and protect information appropriately. They have broad access to sensitive information, so trust depends on handling it responsibly.

5.1 Use of Information
  • Follow the organization's policies, procedures and applicable laws on the use of information.
  • Must not use information for personal gain or in a manner contrary to the law or detrimental to the organization's legitimate and ethical objectives.
5.2 Protection of Information
  • Be aware of their responsibility for protecting information and demonstrate respect for its value and ownership.
  • Protect information during and after engagements (secure storage, access controls, retention).
  • The CAE ensures policies exist on access, retention and release of engagement records and information shared with external parties.

Disclosure decision guide

  1. Is disclosure required by law or regulation? (e.g., subpoena, regulator request) → disclose as legally required, following policy and legal counsel.
  2. Is the recipient authorized by policy? (board, senior management, external auditors under agreed protocol) → disclose what they need.
  3. Is it an external request not covered by policy? → obtain approval from senior management and/or legal counsel before releasing; CAE decides per policy.
  4. Otherwise → do not disclose. Never use it for personal benefit (e.g., buying shares before an acquisition is announced).
Exam trapConfidentiality does not prevent reporting illegal acts or significant risks to the board; the board is an authorized recipient. It also does not override legal disclosure obligations. Whistleblowing outside the organization is a complex legal matter: follow law and policy and consult legal counsel.

Applying ethics: dilemmas, conflicts of interest and decision steps
Conflict of interestA situation in which an internal auditor who is in a position of trust has a competing professional or personal interest. It can exist even if no unethical or improper act results, because it creates an appearance of impropriety that undermines confidence.

A practical ethical decision process

  1. Recognize the ethical issue and which principle(s) apply (integrity, objectivity, competency, due care, confidentiality).
  2. Gather facts and identify stakeholders affected.
  3. Identify options, including relevant policy, law and the Standards.
  4. Consult: supervisor or CAE; legal counsel; the board for serious issues; The IIA's guidance.

The IIA's Three Lines Model (2020)

The six principles

Principle 1 · Governance

Requires structures and processes for accountability (governing body), actions (management, incl. managing risk), and assurance and advice (independent internal audit).

Principle 2 · Governing body roles

Ensures structures and processes are in place; delegates to management; establishes and oversees an independent internal audit function; accountable to stakeholders.

Principle 3 · Management and first and second line roles

Management is responsible for achieving objectives. First line: delivering products/services and managing risk. Second line: assistance in managing risk (expertise, monitoring, challenge).

Principle 4 · Third line roles

Internal audit provides independent and objective assurance and advice on the adequacy and effectiveness of governance and risk management.

Principle 5 · Third line independence

Independence from management responsibilities is critical to objectivity, authority and credibility: accountability to the governing body, unfettered access, freedom from bias.

Principle 6 · Creating and protecting value

All roles working together contribute to value when aligned with each other and with stakeholders' interests.

Elements of an effective ethics and compliance program

Based on the US Federal Sentencing Guidelines (widely used as a benchmark) and good practice.

  1. Standards and procedures: code of conduct, policies to prevent and detect misconduct.
  2. Governance and oversight: board oversight; a high-level person (e.g., chief ethics & compliance officer) with authority and resources.
  3. Due care in delegation: don't give substantial authority to people with a history of misconduct (background checks).
  4. Communication and training: regular, role-based training; certifications of the code.
  5. Monitoring, auditing and reporting: hotlines/whistleblowing channels with anonymity and non-retaliation.
  6. Incentives and consistent discipline: enforce standards at all levels.
  7. Response and prevention: investigate, remediate root causes, update the program.
Internal audit's roleEvaluate the design and effectiveness of the ethics program and culture (e.g., code of conduct awareness, hotline effectiveness, investigation handling, tone at the top). How long the impact lasts.

Correlation

III.7

Internal audit's role in enterprise risk management

Based on The IIA's position paper "The Role of Internal Auditing in Enterprise-wide Risk Management". The key test: does the role make internal audit responsible for managing risk? If yes, it is not allowed.

Safeguards for the middle zone

  • Management remains responsible for risk management; this is clear in the charter, approved by the board.
  • Internal audit does not manage any risks on management's behalf.
  • Internal audit provides advice, challenge and support to management's decision-making, not the decisions themselves.
  • Internal audit cannot also give objective assurance on any part of the ERM framework it is responsible for; such assurance is provided by other suitably qualified parties.
  • Any work beyond assurance activities is recognized as an advisory engagement and follows the related Standards.
Key exam pointWhen an organization has no risk management process, the CAE should bring this to the board's attention and may recommend establishing one, or act as a facilitator or champion (with safeguards). Internal audit still performs its own risk assessment to build the risk-based plan (Standard 9.4).

Other classifications

Manual vs automated

Automated controls are consistent and efficient once tested (test once + test ITGCs). Manual controls allow judgment but are prone to error and override.

IT general controls (ITGCs) vs application controls

ITGCs apply across systems: access security, change management, program development, computer operations, backup. Application controls apply to specific transactions: input (edit, validation), processing (batch totals), output (reconciliation, distribution).

Key vs secondary controls

Key controls are essential to mitigate a significant risk; if they fail, the risk is not adequately managed. Focus of testing.

Entity-level vs process (transaction) controls

Entity-level: tone at the top, code of conduct, risk assessment, board oversight, period-end reporting. Process-level: approvals and reconciliations in specific cycles.

Feedforward, concurrent, feedback

Management control by timing: before (anticipate problems, e.g., budgets, input checks), during (real-time supervision), after (compare results to standards, e.g., variance reports).

Hard vs soft

Formal mechanisms vs culture and behavior (see III.3).

Segregation of duties (SoD)

Exam traps
  • SoD is preventive. Reconciliation is detective. Training and policies are directive.
  • SoD can be defeated by collusion; job rotation and mandatory vacations help detect it.
  • In small entities where SoD is impossible, the best answer is usually a compensating control such as owner or manager review.

III.10

Evaluating controls: design, operation, limitations and deficiencies
Control (IIA)Any action taken by management, the board and other parties to manage risk and increase the likelihood that established objectives and goals will be achieved. Control processes are the policies, procedures and activities designed and operated to manage risks within the organization's risk tolerance.
Design adequacy

Is the control, if operated as designed, capable of mitigating the risk to an acceptable level? Tested by walkthroughs, inquiry, process mapping, inspection of design documents.

then
Operating effectiveness

Did the control actually operate consistently as designed throughout the period, by competent people? Tested by observation, inspection of evidence, reperformance, sampling.

Key exam pointIf the design is inadequate, there is no point testing operating effectiveness: report the design deficiency. Only well-designed controls are tested for operation.

Objectives internal audit evaluates (governance, risk and control)

Achievement of the organization's strategic objectives

Reliability and integrity of financial and operational information

Effectiveness and efficiency of operations and programs

Safeguarding of assets

Compliance with laws, regulations, policies, procedures and contracts

Inherent limitations of internal control

Human judgment

Faulty decisions under time pressure or limited information.

Breakdowns

Errors, fatigue, misunderstanding of instructions.

Management override

Managers can bypass controls for illegitimate purposes.

Collusion

Two or more people acting together defeat SoD.

Cost vs benefit

A control should not cost more than the benefit (risk reduction) it gives.

External events

Events beyond the organization's control.

For these reasons internal control provides reasonable assurance, never absolute assurance.

ACFE Report to the Nations 2024: patterns to know
  • Organizations lose an estimated 5% of revenue to fraud each year.
  • Tips are the most common detection method (about 43% of cases), more than half from employees. Hotlines speed up detection and reduce losses.
  • Median duration before detection is about 12 months.
  • Higher authority and longer tenure → larger losses. Lack of internal controls and override of existing controls are the top weaknesses.

IV.3

Fraud risk management and responsibilities

COSO / ACFE Fraud Risk Management Guide: five principles

Who is responsible for what

PartyResponsibility
Board / audit committeeOversight of fraud risk; sets tone; monitors management override risk; ensures investigations of senior management are independent
Senior managementPrimary responsibility for designing and implementing fraud prevention and detection controls; fraud risk assessment; culture
Internal auditEvaluates the potential for fraud and how the organization manages fraud risk; considers fraud risks in planning every engagement; may assist or lead investigations if in the mandate; not responsible for preventing fraud
External auditorObtains reasonable assurance that the financial statements are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error (ISA 240 / EAS 240)
EmployeesFollow the code of conduct, comply with controls, report suspicions (whistleblowing)
Fraud investigators / legal / HR / securityInvestigate allegations, preserve evidence, determine legal action and discipline

Fraud risk assessment steps

  1. Identify inherent fraud risks (brainstorm schemes across the fraud tree, incentives, pressures, opportunities, including management override).
  2. Assess the likelihood and significance of each risk.
  3. Identify people and departments at risk and how fraud could be committed and concealed.
  4. Identify and map existing controls to the risks; evaluate design and effectiveness.
  5. Assess residual fraud risk.
  6. Respond: new controls, analytics, audit procedures; document and update periodically.
Internal auditor obligations under the Standards (exam focus)
  • Have sufficient knowledge to evaluate the risk of fraud and how it is managed (Competency).
  • Consider the probability of significant errors, fraud and noncompliance when exercising due professional care (4.2), and fraud risks in every engagement risk assessment (13.2).
  • Report significant risk exposures, including fraud risks, and control issues to the board and senior management.
  • Due professional care does not guarantee that fraud will be found.
Exam trap"Internal audit is responsible for detecting fraud" is wrong. Management is responsible for prevention and detection. Internal auditors must be alert to red flags and evaluate fraud risk management. When they suspect fraud, they inform the appropriate authorities in the organization (per policy) and may recommend an investigation.

IV.4

Red flags, and fraud prevention and detection controls
Red flagAn indicator, condition or symptom that fraud may exist. A red flag is not proof of fraud; it is a reason for further inquiry.
Behavioral red flags
  • Living beyond means (the most common)
  • Financial difficulties, debt, gambling
  • Unusually close association with a vendor or customer
  • Excessive control issues, unwilling to share duties
  • Refuses vacations or promotions
  • Divorce or family problems; irritability, defensiveness
  • "Wheeler-dealer" attitude; complaints about pay
  • Past employment or legal problems
Transactional and accounting red flags
  • Missing or photocopied documents; altered records
  • Duplicate payments; round-dollar amounts; just-below-approval-limit amounts
  • Unusual journal entries (period end, by senior people, to suspense accounts)
  • Vendor with a P.O. box, same address or bank account as an employee
  • Unexplained inventory shortages; excessive write-offs, credits, voids
  • Revenue growth without cash flow growth; unusual margins vs peers
  • Complex or related-party transactions without business purpose
  • Frequent changes of auditors or legal advisors

Fraud prevention controls

Ethical tone & code

Tone at the top, code of conduct with annual certification.

Hiring & screening

Background checks, reference checks for sensitive roles.

Segregation of duties

Authorization, custody, recording, reconciliation.

Authorization limits

Delegation of authority matrix; dual signatures.

Physical & logical access

Locks, safes, user access reviews, least privilege.

Job rotation & mandatory vacations

Expose concealed schemes (lapping, kickbacks).

Fraud awareness training

Employees learn red flags and how to report.

Vendor & customer due diligence

Vendor master file controls, KYC.

Fraud detection controls

Whistleblower hotline

Anonymous, multiple channels, non-retaliation. #1 detection method.

Reconciliations

Bank, inventory, subledgers to GL.

Management review

Variance analysis, exception reports.

Surprise audits & counts

Unannounced cash and inventory counts.

Data analytics & continuous monitoring

Automated tests over full populations (see IV.6).

Internal audit

Evaluates controls; detects red flags during engagements.

Key exam pointPrevention is generally more cost-effective than detection. But visible detective controls also deter fraud: the perception that fraud will be detected is one of the strongest preventive forces.

IV.5

Fraud investigation: process, interviews, evidence and reporting
Fraud investigationA process to resolve allegations or suspicions of fraud: gathering sufficient information to determine whether fraud occurred, the loss, who was involved, and how it happened. It is not a normal assurance engagement and often requires legal counsel.

Investigation process

  1. Receive and evaluate the allegation (predication): is there a reasonable basis to believe fraud occurred? Notify per the fraud policy (legal, management, board if senior people are involved).
  2. Plan: define objectives and scope; assemble a team with the right skills (investigators, IT forensics, legal, HR, security); maintain confidentiality and need-to-know.
  3. Preserve and gather evidence: secure documents and devices; forensic imaging; maintain chain of custody.
  4. Analyze: document review, data analytics, financial tracing, net-worth analysis.
  5. Interview: from least to most culpable (see below).
  6. Report: facts, findings and evidence; no opinion on guilt; recommendations on control weaknesses.
  7. Resolution: management decides on discipline, recovery, prosecution; insurance claims; fix the control gaps (lessons learned).

Interview order and types

Evidence and forensic concepts

Chain of custody

Documented record of who collected, handled and stored each item of evidence, when and where. Breaks can make evidence inadmissible.

Forensic imaging

Bit-by-bit copy of a device, verified with a hash value; analysis done on the copy, using write-blockers.

Forensic accounting

Accounting, auditing and investigative skills applied to legal matters (tracing funds, quantifying losses, expert testimony).

Net worth / lifestyle analysis

Compares known income with increases in assets and spending to estimate unexplained income.

Document examination

Detect forged signatures, altered documents, backdating.

Legal privilege

Investigations directed by legal counsel can protect communications; internal auditors should consult counsel early.

Exam traps
  • The investigator does not conclude whether someone is guilty; the report presents facts. Guilt is for the courts.
  • If fraud involves senior management (including the CEO), report to the board/audit committee, not to the CEO.
  • Do not alert the suspect early; preserve evidence first.
  • Internal auditors should consult legal counsel before investigative steps that could have legal consequences.

Data analytics for fraud detection (including Benford's Law)

Technology lets internal auditors test entire populations instead of samples, and monitor continuously. Most fraud tests look for anomalies, duplicates, gaps and relationships.

Trend & ratio analysisUnusual changes in margins, DSO, inventory turnover vs history and peers

Glossary
Advisory services
Advice to stakeholders without providing assurance or taking on management responsibilities; nature and scope agreed with stakeholders.
Assurance
Statement intended to increase stakeholders' confidence about an organization's governance, risk management and control processes over an issue, condition, subject matter or activity, against criteria.
Board
Highest-level body charged with governance (board of directors, audit committee, supervisory board, council). If none, the group or person charged with governance.
Chief audit executive (CAE)
The leadership role responsible for effectively managing all aspects of the internal audit function and ensuring quality performance per the charter and the Standards. Title varies.
Charter
Formal document that includes the mandate, organizational position, reporting relationships, scope of work, types of services and other specifications.
Compliance
Adherence to laws, regulations, contracts, policies and other requirements.
Conflict of interest
Situation in which a person in a position of trust has a competing professional or personal interest.
Control
Any action taken by management, the board and other parties to manage risk and increase the likelihood that objectives will be achieved.
Control processes
Policies, procedures and activities designed and operated to manage risks within the organization's risk tolerance.
Criteria
Specifications of the desired state of the activity under review (also "evaluation criteria").
Engagement
A specific internal audit assignment or project with specific objectives (assurance or advisory).
Essential conditions
Responsibilities of the board and senior management in Domain III needed for an effective internal audit function.
External service provider
Person or firm outside the organization with special knowledge, skill and experience in a particular discipline.
Finding
The determination that a gap exists between the evaluation criteria and the condition of the activity under review.
Fraud
Intentional act characterized by deceit, concealment, dishonesty, misappropriation, forgery or violation of trust to gain unjust or illegal advantage.
Governance
Combination of processes and structures implemented by the board to inform, direct, manage and monitor activities toward objectives.
Impairment
Condition that undermines independence or objectivity (conflicts of interest, scope limitation, access restrictions, resource limits).
Independence
Freedom from conditions that impair the ability of the IA function to carry out responsibilities in an unbiased manner.
Inherent risk
Combination of internal and external risk factors in their pure, uncontrolled state, absent management actions.
Mandate
The internal audit function's authority, role and responsibilities, granted by the board and/or laws and regulations.
Objectivity
Unbiased mental attitude allowing professional judgments without compromise.
Professional skepticism
Questioning and critically assessing the reliability of information.
Residual risk
The portion of inherent risk remaining after management actions are implemented.
Risk
Positive or negative effect of uncertainty on objectives.
Risk appetite
Types and amount of risk an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its strategies and objectives.
Risk management
Process to identify, assess, manage and control potential events or situations to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives.
Risk tolerance
Acceptable variations in performance related to achieving objectives.
Senior management
Highest level of executive management accountable to the board for executing strategy and objectives (e.g., CEO and direct reports).
Significance
Relative importance of a matter, judged by magnitude, nature, effect, relevance and impact.
Stakeholder
Party with a direct or indirect interest in the organization's activities and outcomes.
Topical requirements
Mandatory requirements setting the minimum baseline for assurance and advisory work on specific risk areas.
Root cause
Core issue or underlying reason for the difference between criteria and condition.
